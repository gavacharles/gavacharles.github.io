Turning
price shocks
into evidence you can act on.

I'm Charles Gavamukulya, a PhD candidate in Construction Management at the University of Johannesburg, working where construction, law and the city meet. Years of contract administration and claims work on Ugandan roads, railways and drainage schemes left me with two questions: why do infrastructure projects run over budget, slip behind schedule and end in dispute, and how can African cities be planned and built to stay resilient, sustainable and affordable as the climate changes?

My aim is to push forward what we know about how this industry really behaves: to explain its patterns rather than simply describe them, and to turn that understanding into tools that engineers, lawyers, financiers and city leaders can use. Drawing on mathematics, machine learning and GeoAI, I follow infrastructure from procurement to completion, and into the cities it serves. I study how construction costs form and move, and how contracts, procurement rules and the law allocate that risk. I examine why projects fall behind schedule, how delay turns into dispute, and what makes resolution fair and timely. And I look beyond the site: at the safety of the buildings we live and work in, and at how flooding, heat, housing and mobility are reshaping African cities. Running through it all is one question: what will it take to build cities that are resilient, affordable and low-carbon, from the drains beneath our streets to the shift to electric transport?

How I tackle it

01

Explain the phenomena

Why do price shocks ripple through material costs? Why do some contract clauses breed disputes, and why do some delays become claims? I use econometrics (vector autoregressions, local projections and regime-switching models) and text mining of audit reports and case law to find the mechanisms behind what practitioners see on site.

02

Measure what matters

Much of the industry's evidence is locked in PDFs and spreadsheets. Rebuilding Uganda's construction price index, publishing weekly cost signals for 18 African countries and creating open datasets on contract risk make those numbers visible, comparable and testable.

03

Predict and optimise

Probabilistic forecasts, explainable machine learning and physics-informed neural networks help anticipate cost escalation, delay and structural failure, and support better decisions on pricing, scheduling and contract design.

04

Plan for sustainable, resilient cities

GeoAI, satellite imagery and open data map flood and drainage risk, urban heat, housing affordability and the climate exposure of building stock, and show where electric bodas, buses and charging fit into Kampala, Kigali and Johannesburg, so planning puts resilience and low-carbon mobility first.

05

Share it openly

Every public project is on GitHub with interactive maps and dashboards, so other researchers can test, challenge and extend the work, and practitioners can use it straight away.
